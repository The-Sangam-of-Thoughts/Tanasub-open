# Layer 0: Academic, Scientific, and Formulaic Writing

Use this layer to build the evidence-bearing structure of research papers, literature reviews, encyclopedic articles, technical explanations, scientific reports, policy briefs, and other rule-bound documents. Apply the requested citation style, disciplinary conventions, schema, or template before these defaults.

## Route the task

Before drafting, record the deliverable, audience, purpose, scope, required sections, length, evidence standard, citation style, and decision the reader must make. Then choose the closest route:

| Route | Primary structure | Evidence emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Academic argument or review | Question → position or synthesis → evidence → counterposition → bounded conclusion | Directly verified scholarship and primary material |
| Scientific or technical report | Objective → method → results → interpretation → limitations → reproducible next step | Methods, data, uncertainty, and exact provenance |
| Encyclopedic explanation | Definition and boundary → development → mechanisms → examples → disputes → current limits | Balanced, scoped synthesis across relevant traditions |
| Formulaic or schema-bound document | Required fields in required order; one function per field | Evidence appropriate to each field; no prose added merely to fill space |

If the requested form supplies a mandatory structure, follow it. Omit an inapplicable section only after naming the functional equivalent or recording why the function does not apply.

## Establish the evidence contract

Write consequential claims only when their support is available and fit for that exact claim. Keep these expression types distinct:

- **Quotation:** exact verified wording with a precise locator and edition or version.
- **Paraphrase:** the source's meaning and scope in new wording, without dropping caveats.
- **Synthesis:** a conclusion drawn from multiple named sources.
- **Inference:** the writer's bounded interpretation, explicitly labelled and separated from what a source states.

Place citations next to the claims they support. Record scope, confidence, exceptions, counterevidence, access limits, and unresolved disagreement where they change interpretation. A citation, populated field, or source count does not prove a claim. Do not invent quotations, translations, facts, examples presented as real, methods, results, or bibliographic details. When support is missing, narrow the claim, label the gap, or stop that part of the draft.

## Match source strength to the claim

Use the five-tier hierarchy as a fitness guide, with Tier 1 strongest and Tier 5 most limited:

| Tier | Typical evidence | Permitted use |
|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 | Direct primary evidence; verified authoritative scholarly, archival, library, standards, or official records | What the inspected item directly establishes; broader causation still needs support |
| Tier 2 | Peer-reviewed research, scholarly books, critical editions, systematic histories | Substantive factual, interpretive, and method claims within the source's scope |
| Tier 3 | Independent professional criticism and documented institutional or industry sources | Professional interpretation, practice, and reception with disclosed limits |
| Tier 4 | Established practitioner, editor, teacher, or craft sources | Experience-based guidance, scoped and triangulated when consequential |
| Tier 5 | Audience response, community documentation, previews, or access-constrained material | Audience reception or narrow contextual signals; never sole support for a universal or consequential factual claim |

Judge authorship, method, editorial control, citations, independence, relevance, currency, conflicts, and access completeness. Multiple editions, syndications, summaries, and copies of one originating source do not create independence. Inspect the edition, version, page, timestamp, dataset, or record actually cited.

## Give every section one job

Open each section with its distinct reader purpose or answer it immediately through the first claim. Do not repeat the introduction, restate definitions, or summarize the preceding section to manufacture continuity. A section should advance at least one of these functions: define a boundary, explain a mechanism, present evidence, compare interpretations, test an exception, diagnose a problem, or specify an action.

For each substantive instructional or explanatory passage, use this sequence where applicable:

1. **Observable mechanism:** name what happens and the conditions that produce it.
2. **Example:** provide a concrete, attributable observation, case, datum, or worked instance.
3. **Effect:** explain the consequence for the reader, system, argument, or result.
4. **Diagnostic:** give a question, measurement, or failure signal that reveals whether the mechanism is operating.
5. **Revision action:** prescribe a bounded change tied to the diagnosis.
6. **Scope:** state exceptions, uncertainty, counterevidence, or a form-specific equivalent.

A definition or recommendation without mechanism is incomplete. An example without analysis is illustration, not proof. A revision action should follow from the diagnosed cause rather than offer generic encouragement.

## Build the document

Use only the modules the task needs, in an order that serves the reader:

1. Purpose, question, practical promise, and boundaries.
2. Definitions, terms, adjacent categories, and exclusions.
3. Historical, theoretical, or methodological context.
4. Evidence or results, organized by the actual questions rather than by source summaries.
5. Mechanisms, comparisons, and concrete examples.
6. Counterevidence, alternative explanations, boundary cases, and limitations.
7. Implications, diagnostics, recommendations, or reproducible next steps.
8. Sources, appendices, data, methods, and cross-links required for verification.

For a full creative-writing research guide, also check the relevant functions from the 16-part architecture: definition and boundaries; history; reader promise; premise and meaning; structure; character or functional equivalent; voice and dialogue; setting; tension and pacing; openings and endings; devices; conventions and innovation; failure modes; exercises; and annotated sources. Do not force all sixteen into a shorter or differently governed deliverable.

## Preserve cultural, regional, and translation scope

Do not present one period, language, region, discipline, or Western tradition as universal. Seek relevant Indian, regional, oral, multilingual, and translated evidence as part of the main analysis rather than as a decorative sidebar. Name the language, region, tradition, community, and transmission route when they matter. Indian evidence must not default to English-language writing or Hindi cinema.

Do not force equivalence between traditions. If no direct equivalent is supported, report the searched scope and access limits, discuss only defensible parallels, and avoid claiming absence. For translated material, name the edition and translator when known, state whether the original or a translation was inspected, and limit claims about sound, rhythm, rhyme, diction, syntax, wordplay, prosody, register, and culturally embedded meaning unless original-language or specialist evidence supports them.

Keep professional criticism separate from audience response. Popularity, ratings, sales, and awards do not establish craft or scholarly quality. For screen and adaptation evidence, distinguish writing-level choices from acting, direction, editing, music, or cinematography unless the analysis explicitly explains their effect on the perception of writing.

## Remove padding and improve access

Apply the anti-padding rule throughout.

Prefer precise verbs, concrete nouns, short paragraphs, descriptive sentence-case headings, and tables only when comparison benefits. Define necessary terms. Use lists only for genuinely parallel items. Make links descriptive and ensure meaning does not depend on color or visual layout.

Delete stock openings, throat-clearing, repeated conclusions, generic praise, symmetrical filler, unsupported universals, and paragraphs that could be moved unchanged to another topic. Never add a weak source, redundant example, or repeated sentence to meet a length, section, or source quota. When evidence is inadequate, preserve the gap instead of padding it.

## Draft and verification loop

1. Build a claim-and-section outline before prose. Assign each section a purpose and each consequential claim its evidence, countercheck, scope, and limitation.
2. Draft from verified notes and data. Keep observations separate from interpretation.
3. Test every section against the mechanism sequence and remove content that performs no distinct function.
4. Verify 100% of quotations, translations, dates, identities, numerical assertions, disputed or limited-confidence claims, and absence claims. Sample other consequential claims as the governing method requires.
5. Check references, terminology, units, tables, figures, equations, links, required fields, and cross-references.
6. Record unresolved limitations and stop conditions. Automated or structural success does not establish factual accuracy, independent acceptance, or publication approval.

Before handoff, provide a compact section-purpose matrix with: heading; reader purpose; claim and evidence IDs or citations; example or observation; diagnostic output; revision action; functional equivalent where needed; and limitation. Report author checks truthfully and leave independent review or human approval pending until actually recorded.
