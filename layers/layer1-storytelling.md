# Layer 1: Storytelling and Engagement

Use this layer after the structural draft and before humanization. It helps a reader enter, follow, feel, and remember the piece. It does not excuse weak evidence, manufactured urgency, clickbait, or a story that obscures the point.

## 1. Set the engagement job

Write one sentence for each:

- **Reader:** Who is reading, in what setting, with what prior knowledge?
- **Need:** What question, tension, or decision brought them here?
- **Change:** What should they understand, feel, or be able to do afterward?
- **Truth boundary:** Which claims require evidence, qualification, or uncertainty?

Then choose one opening pattern and, if useful, one larger story shape. Do not stack formulas merely because they are available.

## 2. Pick one opening pattern

| Pattern | Use when | Sequence | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AIDA** | Persuasive copy needs a clear path to action | Attention → Interest → Desire → Action | A planning model, not a proven universal reading sequence. Earn attention; do not bait it. |
| **PAS** | The reader already recognizes a concrete problem | Problem → deepen the real consequence → Solution | “Agitate” means clarify stakes. Never invent fear, shame, or urgency. |
| **BAB** | A transformation is easier to grasp as contrast | Before → After → Bridge | Make the “after” plausible and name the actual bridge. |
| **Open question** | The answer can be delivered soon and matters | Establish a specific unanswered question → develop → answer | Do not call every curiosity gap a Zeigarnik effect; the original research concerned interrupted tasks and memory. |
| **APP** | A search-led article must confirm relevance quickly | Agree with a defensible reader concern → Promise a bounded benefit → Preview the route | A practitioner mnemonic associated with Backlinko. Promise only what the piece delivers. |
| **Direct lead** | News, technical, urgent, or skeptical readers want the point first | Main finding or answer → essential context → detail | Prefer this inverted-pyramid route when suspense would waste the reader’s time. |

Opening check: after three sentences, can the reader identify the subject, the stake, and why the next paragraph is worth reading?

## 3. Pick one story shape when the material needs one

| Shape | Working beats | Best fit | Do not force it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fall–rise** (“Man in a Hole”) | stable state → trouble → struggle → improved state | Recovery, case study, troubleshooting | the outcome stayed mixed or worsened |
| **Rise–fall–rise** (“Cinderella”) | constraint → opportunity → reversal → durable gain | Transformation with a real setback | chronology or causation would be distorted |
| **Encounter arc** (“Boy Meets Girl”) | encounter → changed fortune → separation/friction → new relation | Relationship, collaboration, idea-meets-context | the labels would erase the actual people or culture |
| **Heroic cycle** | separation → initiation → return | Mythic or transformational narrative | a different tradition or non-returning structure fits better |
| **Story spine** | situation → routine → disruption → consequences → resolution | Fast outline, pitch, children’s story, case narrative | repeated “because of that” links are not genuinely causal |
| **StoryBrand SB7®** | character → problem → guide → plan → call → failure stakes → success | Customer-facing message where the customer is protagonist | literary analysis, neutral reporting, or public information should not become a sales script |

Vonnegut’s named shapes are useful sketches of changing fortune, not a complete taxonomy. Campbell’s monomyth is a comparative interpretive model; the familiar twelve-stage screenwriting outline is a later adaptation. The story-spine prompt was popularized in a thread by former Pixar story artist Emma Coats, but it is not a Pixar corporate law and has earlier teaching antecedents. StoryBrand is Donald Miller’s commercial framework and trademark.

## 4. Build scenes and explanations from observable change

Use **showing** when the reader should infer or experience something: choose an action, sensory detail, line of dialogue, measured result, or consequential choice. Use **telling** for compression, orientation, transitions, and facts that do not merit a scene.

For an important moment, try:

```text
abstract label → observable sign → reader inference
“She was anxious” → she checks the same timestamp twice → anxiety without explanation
“The rollout failed” → error rate rose from 1% to 18% after the flag → failure with evidence
```

Do not decorate every claim with sensory detail. Ask what the chosen detail proves and whether the reader needs to see the moment or simply know it happened.

## 5. Control rhythm and voice

- Vary sentence length when meaning, pace, or emphasis changes. A short sentence can land a point. A longer one can carry relationship, qualification, or accumulation.
- Diagnose monotony across a paragraph; do not obey a mechanical short-medium-long cycle or a sentence-length quota.
- Prefer concrete, familiar verbs when they are more precise. Do not treat words of a particular linguistic origin as inherently better.
- Use contractions when the audience and register permit them.
- Read the draft aloud. Replace phrasing you would not naturally say to this audience, while preserving necessary technical terms.
- If a paragraph is remote from speech, explain its point aloud to an imagined informed friend, then rewrite from that explanation. This adapts Paul Graham’s read-aloud/friend practice; “Coffee Shop Test” is only an informal project label.
- A one-line bridge such as “Here is the catch” may reset attention in informal copy. Use these “bucket brigade” transitions only when they express the real relation; repeated stock bridges sound manufactured.

Gary Provost’s advice supports purposeful sentence-length variation, but the widely reposted demonstration is copyrighted text. Apply the principle; do not imitate or reproduce the passage.

## 6. Design for useful sharing

Berger’s **STEPPS** framework is a six-question lens:

| Lens | Ask |
|---|---|
| Social currency | Does sharing help the reader express a valued identity without exploiting status anxiety? |
| Triggers | What recurring situation will bring this idea back to mind? |
| Emotion | Is the feeling earned by the material and appropriate to the subject? |
| Public | Is the behavior or result observable enough to learn from? |
| Practical value | Can the reader use or pass on something specific? |
| Stories | Is the central idea integral to the story, or can the anecdote travel without it? |

Research on New York Times articles and experiments linked sharing with high-arousal emotions such as awe, anger, and anxiety, while low-arousal sadness was shared less in that setting. This is contextual evidence about social transmission, not an instruction to provoke anger or a guarantee of virality. Never intensify trauma, health risks, or public danger for engagement.

Use **loss framing** only when a real downside is material to the reader's decision: name the plausible loss, its evidence, and the action that can reduce it. Keep gain and loss comparisons fair. Never manufacture fear or scarcity, inflate certainty, or imply that prospect-theory findings guarantee how this audience will respond.

## 7. Write and score the headline

Draft at least three truthful candidates using different structures:

1. **Direct benefit:** `[Useful outcome] without [credible obstacle]`
2. **How-to:** `How to [specific result] when [constraint]`
3. **Specific list:** `[Number] [tools/questions/examples] for [reader job]`
4. **Question:** the exact question the body answers; avoid a question whose truthful answer is merely “no”
5. **Finding or tension:** `[Unexpected finding]: what it changes for [audience]`

Score each candidate from 0–2 on AWAI’s **4 U’s**: useful, urgent, unique, and ultra-specific. Treat urgency as timely relevance, never false scarcity. Also score fidelity: does the body deliver the headline’s exact claim? Choose the clearest high-fidelity candidate, even if it has a lower promotional score.

## 8. Support two reading tracks

For web and business formats, use **Dual-track reading**: let scanners get the argument from the headline, deck, headings, first sentences, tables, and callouts. Let close readers find evidence, reasoning, examples, and qualifications beneath them. When urgency favors the answer first, combine this with the **inverted pyramid**. Each heading should state a useful distinction; it should not merely tease.

## 9. Exit check

- One opening pattern serves the reader’s actual need.
- Any narrative shape preserves chronology, causality, culture, and uncertainty.
- Concrete details carry important moments; summary compresses the rest.
- Sentence rhythm follows meaning rather than a quota.
- Emotional force is earned and ethically proportionate.
- Headline and promise match the delivered content.
- The main idea survives skimming and rewards close reading.
- Named commercial or practitioner frameworks are attributed and remain optional.
