# Storytelling and Engagement: Evidence Dossier

This dossier supports `layers/layer1-storytelling.md`. It separates historical or empirical evidence from commercial frameworks and working heuristics. None of these devices is a universal law. Choose a technique because it fits the reader, material, ethics, and format.

## Evidence labels

- **Research:** a primary study or scholarly historical analysis supports a bounded claim.
- **Primary/official:** the creator, rights holder, publisher, or institution documents the concept.
- **Practitioner:** a working method taught in writing or marketing practice; effectiveness is context-dependent.
- **Heuristic:** a project-authored operational rule synthesized from sources and editorial judgment.

## Opening and persuasion patterns

### AIDA — practitioner model with disputed history

**Working use:** Attention → Interest → Desire → Action. It can expose a missing persuasive transition: a striking opening without relevance, useful information without motivation, or desire without a feasible next action.

AIDA belongs to a family of early advertising “hierarchy of effects” models. The familiar claim that E. St. Elmo Lewis invented the completed formula in 1898 is not secure. Akinori Iwamoto’s historical study found that the conventional attribution rests on weak evidence and identifies important contributions by Frank H. Dukesmith and Arthur Frederick Sheldon. The skill therefore names AIDA without assigning sole invention to Lewis.

**Limits:** AIDA is a planning sequence, not proof that every reader moves linearly through four mental states. It suits concise persuasive material better than neutral analysis or complex recurring decisions.

Sources:

- Akinori Iwamoto, “The Origin of AIDA: Who Invented and Formulated the AIDA Model?” ([open paper](https://ojs.library.carleton.ca/index.php/pcharm/article/download/4340/3310); [journal record and DOI](https://doi.org/10.51102/jmhr.3.2_53)).

### PAS — practitioner heuristic

**Working use:** identify a real problem, make its consequences concrete, then present a proportionate solution. “Agitation” should add causal detail or stakes, not fear, humiliation, or invented urgency.

No primary source establishing a single originator was verified for this implementation. PAS is therefore included as a widely circulated direct-response heuristic, without an invention claim or research-backed efficacy claim.

**Diagnostic:** if the reader does not already recognize the problem, PAS can sound accusatory. If the solution appears miraculous, the bridge is missing.

### BAB — practitioner heuristic

**Working use:** Before → After → Bridge. State the current condition, a credible changed condition, and the mechanism connecting them. It is useful for case studies, onboarding, and transformation copy.

An authoritative origin record was not verified. Copyhackers is often associated with contemporary teaching of the formula, but this dossier does not claim authorship. The method should be treated as a compact contrast structure.

**Diagnostic:** delete an “after” state that promises more than the bridge can produce.

### Open questions and the Zeigarnik boundary

Writers often open a question, delay its answer, and call the device an “open loop.” That rhetorical practice can sustain attention when the question is specific, relevant, and answered on time.

The Zeigarnik effect originated in research about memory for interrupted or incomplete tasks. It does not by itself prove that withholding an article’s answer improves engagement. Later studies have examined interruption under differing conditions, and results should not be flattened into a marketing law.

**Heuristic:** establish a knowledge gap, provide meaningful progress, and close it before frustration exceeds value. Never withhold safety-critical or decision-critical information.

Sources:

- Bluma Zeigarnik, “On Finished and Unfinished Tasks” (1927), commonly indexed through later translations and reviews.
- Schiffman and Greist-Bousquet, “The effect of task interruption and closure on perceived duration” ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03330382)), an example of later boundary research.

### APP and bucket brigades — practitioner devices

The **APP** mnemonic is commonly associated with Brian Dean/Backlinko: Agree, Promise, Preview. Its practical value is rapid relevance confirmation in search-led articles. An exact surviving first-party Backlinko page documenting the formulation was not located in this research pass; attribution should remain cautious.

“Bucket brigade” is a copywriting label for a short transition designed to pull attention into the next sentence. The underlying editorial move is ordinary: signal a turn, complication, example, or consequence. Stock phrases repeated on schedule become another form of padding.

Use both as revision prompts, not required surface patterns. [Backlinko’s official history](https://backlinko.com/about-backlinko) confirms that Brian Dean founded the site, but it is not primary evidence that he originated either device.

## Story shapes

### Vonnegut’s shapes of stories

Kurt Vonnegut famously graphed a protagonist’s fortune over narrative time and used informal names such as “Man in a Hole,” “Boy Meets Girl,” and “Cinderella.” The graphs are memorable diagnostic sketches: where does fortune turn, and how sharply? They are not a complete taxonomy and cannot substitute for event, character, cultural, or causal analysis.

**Use:** draw the actual fortune curve after outlining. If the curve and claimed shape disagree, trust the story. Do not force a recovery ending because a template promises one.

**Provenance caution:** Vonnegut’s University of Chicago anthropology thesis was rejected and is often confused with later lectures and publications. Consult the archival record and Vonnegut’s own presentations before quoting or reproducing graphs. The project synthesizes the idea without copying copyrighted diagrams.

Sources:

- University of Illinois archival handle associated with Vonnegut material: [hdl.handle.net/2142/34799](https://hdl.handle.net/2142/34799).
- Andrew Reagan et al., “The emotional arcs of stories are dominated by six basic shapes,” an empirical computational treatment ([EPJ Data Science, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1140/epjds/s13688-016-0093-1)). Its sentiment trajectories are evidence about that corpus and method, not proof that only six plots exist.

### Campbell’s monomyth / hero’s journey

In *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*, Joseph Campbell presents a comparative monomyth organized broadly as separation, initiation, and return, with possible variations inside those movements. The well-known twelve-stage screenwriting sequence belongs to later adaptation, especially Christopher Vogler; it should not be labeled Campbell’s exact original outline.

**Use:** choose the broad cycle when a protagonist leaves a known order, changes through ordeal or encounter, and returns with a consequential difference. The return matters. Treat the model as one comparative lens, not a universal grammar. It can distort narratives centered on community, recurrence, stasis, dispersal, or traditions whose meanings do not fit a Western individual-hero template.

Sources:

- Joseph Campbell Foundation, [“Joseph Campbell and the Hero’s Journey”](https://www.jcf.org/learn/joseph-campbell-heros-journey) (official foundation explanation).
- Joseph Campbell Foundation, [work record for *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*](https://www.jcf.org/works/titles/the-hero-with-a-thousand-faces/).

### Story spine and the “Pixar” label

The circulated spine runs from an initial situation and routine through disruption, causal consequences, climax, and a changed condition. Former Pixar story artist Emma Coats popularized a compressed version in her #storybasics posts. Reporting from the period records earlier teaching antecedents and warns that “Pixar’s 22 Rules” was an audience-applied label rather than a corporate rulebook.

**Use:** test causality. Replace each link with “therefore” or “because of that.” If the events are connected only by “and then,” the spine has found a plot gap.

Source:

- Aerogramme Writers’ Studio, [“The Story Spine: Pixar’s 4th Rule of Storytelling”](https://www.aerogrammestudio.com/2013/03/22/the-story-spine-pixars-4th-rule-of-storytelling/), including contemporaneous attribution discussion. Treat this as provenance reporting, not experimental evidence.

### StoryBrand SB7®

Donald Miller’s commercial StoryBrand framework positions a customer as the character, a brand as guide, and a product or service as part of a plan. Its seven elements are character, problem, guide, plan, call to action, failure stakes, and success.

**Use:** clarify customer-facing messaging. Check whether the customer’s goal appears before the organization’s biography, whether the plan is legible, and whether success and failure claims are credible.

**Limits and rights:** StoryBrand and SB7 are commercial identifiers. This project provides a short attributed summary and does not reproduce worksheets, certification material, or book text. The framework is not an empirical universal of narrative.

Source:

- StoryBrand, [*Your Brand Is Not the Hero* official PDF](https://storybrand.com/downloads/your-brand-is-not-the-hero.pdf).

## Showing, telling, and concrete evidence

“Show, don’t tell” is useful when rewritten as a choice about reader experience. Showing supplies observable action, sensory evidence, dialogue, thought, or consequence from which a reader infers. Telling names, summarizes, or compresses. Strong prose uses both.

The familiar “moon” quotation attributed to Anton Chekhov is not a reliable verbatim quotation. A genuine letter advises selecting small, grouped details that let a reader form a picture. The craft principle survives without repeating the misquotation.

**Decision rule:** show the moments whose inference, embodiment, or emotional timing matters. Tell connective time, routine action, established facts, and material whose full enactment would waste attention. In technical and academic writing, a measured observation can perform the same evidentiary function as a concrete scene.

Sources:

- University of Worcester, [teaching discussion of showing and telling](https://www.worc.ac.uk/about/news/academic-blog/humanities-blogs/show-dont-tell.aspx).
- The attribution distinction is summarized in [“Show, don’t tell”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Show%2C_don%27t_tell); verify a scholarly edition of Chekhov’s letters before quoting the letter itself.

## Rhythm and conversational voice

### Sentence rhythm

Gary Provost’s *100 Ways to Improve Your Writing* argues for varying sentence length to create rhythm. The much-reposted demonstration is copyrighted and is not reproduced here.

The useful operation is semantic: align syntax with the work a sentence performs. Short sentences can mark decision, shock, or closure. Longer syntax can express qualification, sequence, or accumulation. Fragments can work in informal or dramatic prose; they may be wrong in a method section or legal instruction.

There is no verified “three-length rule” requiring a repeating short-medium-long sequence. Sentence-length standard deviation is at most a diagnostic for flatness, not a quality target.

Source:

- Penguin Random House, [official publisher record for Gary Provost’s *100 Ways to Improve Your Writing (Updated)*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/602155/100-ways-to-improve-your-writing-updated-by-gary-provost/).

### Familiar verbs and register

Concrete verbs often reduce nominalization and make agency visible: “the committee rejected” is clearer than “a rejection was undertaken.” Some style advice calls this a preference for “Anglo-Saxon verbs.” That label is a poor operational rule: etymology does not determine clarity, and technical or culturally specific vocabulary may be exact.

**Heuristic:** prefer the most precise word the intended reader can process without needless friction. Preserve terms of art when they carry necessary meaning.

### Paul Graham’s read-aloud test

Paul Graham advises writers to ask whether they would say a sentence to a friend, read drafts aloud, and replace language that does not sound conversational. If sentence-level repair is insufficient, he suggests explaining the idea to a friend and rebuilding from that explanation.

“Coffee Shop Test” is this project’s informal shorthand, not Graham’s named framework. Spoken naturalness must still respect genre: a grant method, judgment, API reference, and personal essay should not sound identical.

Source:

- Paul Graham, [“Write Like You Talk”](https://www.paulgraham.com/talk.html) (author’s essay, 2015).

## Engagement, emotion, and sharing

### STEPPS

Jonah Berger’s *Contagious* organizes six drivers of word of mouth and sharing as Social Currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical Value, and Stories. Use them as questions about transmission, not ingredients that guarantee it.

The “Stories” check is especially important: the information or brand must be integral to the narrative. A memorable anecdote that sheds its intended message has failed strategically even if people repeat it.

Sources:

- Jonah Berger, [official *Contagious* page](https://jonahberger.com/books/contagious/) and [resources](https://jonahberger.com/contagious-resources/).
- Knowledge at Wharton, [author interview explaining STEPPS](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/contagious-jonah-berger-on-why-things-catch-on/).

### Emotional arousal and social transmission

Berger and Katherine Milkman analyzed New York Times articles and conducted experiments. Their results linked sharing with arousal: awe and some high-arousal negative emotions, including anger and anxiety, were associated with greater transmission; sadness, a lower-arousal emotion in their model, was associated with less. Practical usefulness, interest, and surprise were also positively related to virality in the field data.

**Limits:** the work studied particular content, platforms, measures, and experiments. It does not establish that emotional intensity improves comprehension, trust, conversion, or public value. It gives no ethical license to inflame readers. For grief, health, conflict, and vulnerable audiences, proportionality and accuracy outrank sharing.

Source:

- Jonah Berger and Katherine L. Milkman, “What Makes Online Content Viral?” *Journal of Marketing Research* 49(2), 192–205 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1509/JMR.10.0353)).

### Loss framing

Prospect theory research by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that choices can change when equivalent outcomes are framed as gains or losses, alongside other findings about decision under risk. “Loss aversion” is often generalized far beyond the tested decisions.

**Use:** when a real downside is decision-relevant, state it concretely and compare it fairly with the upside. Do not fabricate scarcity, imply certainty, or turn a descriptive behavioral finding into a command to frighten readers.

Source:

- Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, “Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk,” *Econometrica* 47(2), 263–291 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185)).

## Headlines and entry architecture

### Five reusable structures

1. **Direct benefit:** state the valuable outcome and credible constraint.
2. **How-to:** promise a process for a specific result.
3. **Specific list:** name the kind and number of useful items.
4. **Question:** ask what the body actually answers.
5. **Finding/tension:** lead with a supported result or consequential contradiction.

These are project-level heuristics, not empirically ranked formulas. Draft several because comparison exposes vague nouns, inflated promises, and hidden audience assumptions.

### AWAI’s 4 U’s

AWAI documents the Four U’s as useful, urgent, unique, and ultra-specific, and attributes their development to Michael Masterson and Bill Bonner. The framework began in direct-response practice and is an editorial scorecard, not a validated prediction model.

Use “urgent” to mean timely or consequential. A durable guide may properly score zero on urgency. Add a fifth project criterion—**fidelity**—so promotional strength cannot outrank truth.

Sources:

- AWAI, [“A Review of the 4 U’s”](https://www.awai.com/2001/06/a-review-of-the-4-us/) (2001).
- AWAI, [current explanation of the 4 U’s](https://www.awai.com/2024/04/write-an-attention-getting-headline/).

## Long-form web structures

### Inverted pyramid

Give the most decision-relevant answer first, then essential context, supporting detail, and background. This structure respects readers who may stop early and suits news, updates, incident communication, and technical answers. It can coexist with narrative inside later sections.

### APP introduction

Confirm a defensible concern, promise a bounded payoff, and preview the route. Avoid fake agreement (“we all know”), impossible promises, and table-of-contents prose that delays substance.

### Dual-track reading

This is a project-authored synthesis for web readability. Build a scan track from the title, deck, headings, first sentences, tables, and callouts. Build a close-reading track from reasoning, evidence, examples, counterevidence, and qualifications. The tracks must make the same claim at different resolutions.

## Selection matrix

| Situation | Opening | Shape | Supporting lens |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technical answer | Direct lead | Usually none | Inverted pyramid + concrete evidence |
| Academic explanation | Claim/question | Evidence-led argument | Show through data/example; cautious emotion |
| Case study | BAB or direct result | Fall–rise if truthful | Observable before/after measures |
| Ethical sales page | AIDA or PAS | StoryBrand if appropriate | 4 U’s + fidelity; no invented loss |
| Search-led guide | APP or direct lead | Usually none | Dual-track reading + practical value |
| Personal essay | Scene or open question | Actual emotional arc | Rhythm + read-aloud test |
| Fiction outline | Scene/question | One chosen narrative lens | Causality, concrete detail, cultural fit |
| Social post | Direct tension or benefit | Micro fall–rise only if real | One STEPPS lens; platform constraint |

## Final evidence discipline

Before using a named framework, record:

1. whether it is research, official/commercial guidance, practitioner advice, or a heuristic;
2. which result the source actually supports;
3. what is extrapolation;
4. whether a trademark or copyrighted expression requires attribution;
5. what would make the device inappropriate for this reader or topic.

Engagement is successful when it directs attention toward truthful value. Attention without fidelity is a failure.
