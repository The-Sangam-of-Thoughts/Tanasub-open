# Compassionate — Research Dossier

- Tone ID: `tone-compassionate`
- Family: Candor
- Researched: 2026-09-24
- Evidence status: complete (one item flagged evidence-incomplete, four stated limits, see §I)

Scope of this tone: the narrator's voice carries **care expressed inside another's
suffering**. Stakes belong to the genre engine; texture belongs to the tone. The
hard boundary the repository enforces: this tone is care for *someone else's*
pain, not general affiliative kindness (`tone-warm`) and not the narrator's own
mourning (`tone-elegiac`). A recognition test for the card author: *is there a
sufferer other than the speaker, and is the speaker's attention on that sufferer's
dignity rather than on the speaker's own feeling?* If the second half fails, the
tone has collapsed into saviorism or sentimentality (§G).

## B. Cognitive and psychological foundations

**Compassion is not empathy (Singer & Klimecki 2014; Goetz et al. 2010).**
Singer and Klimecki distinguish empathy (resonance with another's affect, which
can be vicarious *distress*) from compassion (a feeling of concern coupled with
the motivation to improve the other's welfare). Goetz, Keltner and Simon-Thomas
synthesize the evolutionary and empirical record: compassion has a distinct
appraisal structure (suffering is serious, undeserved, and remediable), a
distinct physiology, and a prosocial action tendency. Craft consequence: the
voice should carry the *motivation toward* the sufferer — attention, steadiness,
small practical regard — not merely echo the pain. A narrator who only mirrors
anguish is inside the empathy/personal-distress pole, which is not this tone
(`clm-tone-compassionate-distinct-from-empathy`).

**Training separates the two.** Klimecki et al. (2014, *SCAN* 9(6):873–879)
found that empathy training increased negative affect and distress-related
activation, whereas subsequent compassion training increased positive affect and
prosocial behaviour; this is the strongest evidence that the two states are
dissociable and that compassion is trainable. Caveat: laboratory tasks do not
license a claim that a written voice *causes* the reader's compassion; the
mechanism is transferred by analogy, so the card must state scope.

**Perspective-taking (Davis 1983; Batson 1991).** Davis's multidimensional
measure separates *perspective-taking*, *empathic concern*, *fantasy*, and
*personal distress*; the constructs behave differently. Batson's empathy-altruism
program shows that instructed perspective-taking toward a specific person raises
empathic concern and helping, whereas the personal-distress route motivates
escape. Craft consequence: get the reader into *one* sufferer's viewpoint
(interiority, sensory particularity) rather than describing suffering from
outside; that is the perspective-taking operator
(`clm-tone-compassionate-perspective-taking`).

**The identifiable victim and psychic numbing (Small & Loewenstein 2003; Slovic
2007).** Small and Loewenstein found greater generosity toward an identified
individual than toward a statistical victim with identical need. Slovic's
"psychic numbing" account holds that affect does not scale: as the number of
victims rises, feeling flattens, so mass figures fail to move action. Both are
directly load-bearing for the tone: the voice individuates
(`clm-tone-compassionate-identifiable-victim`). Counterevidence is recorded —
Kogut and Ritov (2005) found the "identified victim" effect tracked *singular*
identification, not group identification, which is a boundary, not a refutation.

**Compassion fatigue and its discontents (Figley 1995; Sinclair et al. 2017).**
Figley framed compassion fatigue as the cost of caring, a secondary traumatic
stress in helpers. Sinclair and colleagues' meta-narrative review shows the
construct is used inconsistently and often conflated with burnout and vicarious
trauma. This makes the *dosage* claim honest rather than pop-psychological: the
narrator who holds the compassionate register indefinitely risks producing
numbing in the reader and, in a real brief, misrepresenting care as limitless
(`clm-tone-compassionate-compassion-fatigue-yield`).

**Adjunct: self-compassion (Neff 2003).** Neff's scale separates self-kindness,
common humanity, and mindfulness from self-criticism. It is not the tone (the
tone is care for *another*), but "common humanity" supplies the linguistic move
of shared vulnerability that keeps compassion from becoming condescension. Not a
consequential claim here; noted for the card author.

## C. Rhetorical and literary tradition

**Consolation (Greco-Roman and Latin).** The *consolatio* tradition — Crantor's
lost *Peri penthous*, Seneca's *ad Helviam*, and Boethius's *Consolation of
Philosophy* (c. 524) — is the West's oldest sustained practice of care inside
suffering. Boethius's structure is instructive: a prisoner in despair is met by
Philosophy, who does not begin with argument or reproach but with diagnosis of
the illness and a graduated return of the sufferer's own reason. The reframing
(the impermanence of Fortune, the good) comes *after* the grief is acknowledged.
The failure the tradition itself names is false consolation: telling the sufferer
that the loss does not matter. Modern reception of the *consolatio* genre is
contested precisely on this point — consolation can shade into theodicy that
silences complaint (`clm-tone-compassionate-consolation-presence`; §G).

**Pastoral and clinical care.** Clebsch and Jaekle's history of pastoral care
traces a shift across epochs (healing, sustaining, guiding, reconciling) and
shows that the sustaining function — staying with the person — is the historic
core. Rogers's client-centred conditions (congruence, unconditional positive
regard, accurate empathic understanding) gave the twentieth century its
operational vocabulary: care is a stance, not a technique of advice. This is the
direct tradition behind "pastoral-care language" and it is why the tone must not
prescribe (`clm-tone-compassionate-pastoral-care-conditions`).

**Witness and testimony.** Felman and Laub's *Testimony* (1992) theorizes
witnessing as the listener's co-responsibility for a truth that the sufferer
cannot fully carry alone; Sontag's *Regarding the Pain of Others* (2003) warns
that images of atrocity can produce both solidarity and numbing, and that
spectatorship is not the same as understanding. The tradition supplies the
tone's central formal problem: how to represent another's suffering without
consuming it.

**What remains contested.** Whether consolation can avoid paternalism; whether
compassion is a universal emotion or a culturally shaped virtue; and whether the
tone should display the narrator's feeling at all, or stay invisible so the
sufferer dominates the page. This dossier takes the last position as a default,
not a law.

## E. Cross-cultural survey

**Indian — *karuna rasa* (aesthetic) and *karuna* (ethical).** These are related
but not identical, and the card must not merge them. In Bharata's *Natyashastra*
(Ch. 6, trans. Ghosh 1951) *karuna* is one of the principal rasas: the
compassionate sentiment is generated from the sufferer's situation through
determinants, consequents, and transient states, and is *generalized* into
aesthetic relish rather than left as private grief. Abhinavagupta's commentary
(Ingalls/Masson/Patwardhan 1990) deepens this: rasa is tasted by a spectator
whose own ego is momentarily suspended. Craft consequence: the Indian tradition
sanctions the sufferer as a *shared* aesthetic object, which is precisely where
the modern ethics of representation pushes back (§G). Separately, Buddhist
*karuna* (below) is a cultivated virtue, not a rasa. Translation limit: Sanskrit
aesthetic terms do not map cleanly onto "compassion"; this dossier leans on the
critical editions, not on loose English glosses
(`clm-tone-compassionate-karuna-rasa`).

**Indian/Buddhist — *karuna* as a *brahmavihara*.** Buddhaghosa's *Visuddhimagga*
(trans. Ñāṇamoli 1975) lists *karuna* among the four sublime abidings, defines it
as the wish that others be free of suffering, and — crucially for tone — names
its "near enemy" as grief or sentimental attachment, and its "far enemy" as
cruelty. Śāntideva's *Bodhicaryāvatāra* (trans. Wallace 1997) supplies the
rhetorical machinery: the equalizing of self and other and the exchanging of
self for other, which ground an impartial compassion. Modern contemplative
science (Lutz et al. 2008) finds that compassion meditation alters emotional
reactivity and prosociality, which is at least consistent with the tradition's
claim that compassion is trainable. Buddhist compassion is therefore strong
evidence against equating the tone with pity: it is universal in scope and
obligation-bearing (`clm-tone-compassionate-buddhist-karuna-universal`).

**Chinese — Confucian *ren* and Mencian extension.** The *Mengzi* (trans. Van
Norden 2008) grounds benevolence in a spontaneous reaction to suffering: anyone
seeing a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and compassion, not for
advantage or reputation (2A6). Mencius calls compassion the "sprout" of *ren*
(6A6) and argues it must be "extended" from the near case to the distant — the
king's compassion for an ox must be extended to his suffering subjects (1A7).
The SEP entry (2024) synthesizes the scholarship. Craft consequence: this is a
narrative theory of moral attention, and it is directly usable — start from a
concrete near case and *extend* the reader's concern. Limit: *ren* is a broader
virtue than the English "compassion" and is bound to a Confucian role ethic, so
it must not be universalized into a generic compassion
(`clm-tone-compassionate-confucian-ren-extension`).

**Latin/Roman — Boethian *consolatio*.** The *Consolation of Philosophy* (c. 524)
is a non-Anglophone model of care expressed inside suffering, and it is the
tradition's most-cited structural template. It is included here as a fourth,
non-Anglophone tradition (§F `work-boethius-consolation`), with the caveat that
its consoler is allegorical Reason and its theology is specifically late-antique.

**No false universal.** *Karuna*, *ren*, and *consolatio* do not converge on a
single technique or a single theory of emotion. They differ on whether the
sufferer is a spectator's aesthetic object (Indian rasa), a neighbour whose
concern must be extended (Confucian), or a soul to be re-oriented (Boethian).
The card states the family resemblance and the differences.

## G. Failure and misuse research

**Pity tourism / poverty porn.** Cole's *Atlantic* essay (2012) names the
"white-savior industrial complex": helping narrated as an emotional experience
that validates the helper's privilege, with the helped reduced to "hungry
mouths." Bex and Craps (2016) contrast humanitarian testimony that preserves
agency with advocacy that makes the sufferer a backdrop for Western heroism.
Diagnostic: delete every sentence about the helper's feeling; if the sufferer
disappears, the text was about the helper
(`clm-tone-compassionate-saviorism-witness-ethics`).

**Saviorism.** The rescue frame casts the sufferer as passive and the narrator as
agent. The Mencian and Buddhist traditions both push the other way: compassion
is a *wish* for the other's good, not the assumption that the speaker is the
solution. Diagnostic: count verbs where the sufferer is the grammatical subject
of an action versus the object of one.

**Condescension and speaking over.** Spivak's "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988)
and Sontag's *Regarding the Pain of Others* (2003) document the epistemic harm of
representing others from a position that cannot hear them. Adichie's "single
story" (shared `src-adichie-single-story-2009`) names the flattening effect.
Diagnostic: does any sufferer in the draft hold and voice a perspective, or are
they only described?

**Minimization masquerading as consolation.** The Boethian frame, misapplied,
becomes "everything happens for a reason." The consolation tradition itself
models the remedy: in the *Consolation*, reframing follows acknowledgment.
Diagnostic: if a reframe sentence can be moved *before* the disclosure of pain
without loss, the draft has not earned it.

**Compassion fatigue and numbing.** Sinclair et al. (2017) show the construct is
real but conceptually muddy; Slovic (2007) shows that scale increases numbing.
Diagnostic: after three consecutive suffering vignettes of similar intensity, a
reader habituates; the draft needs a change of register or a concrete action
(`clm-tone-compassionate-compassion-fatigue-yield`, `clm-tone-compassionate-dosage-saturation`).

**Sentimentality / melodrama.** Emotion that exceeds the established stakes
(Gardner 1984, shared) reads as the narrator's self-indulgence, not care for the
sufferer. Diagnostic: remove the emotive adjectives; if nothing consequential is
lost, the emotion was manufactured.

## H. Dosage and saturation findings

1. **Affect does not scale.** Slovic (2007): feeling flattens as the number of
   victims grows, so piling up statistics suppresses compassion. Rule: one
   individuated sufferer per evidentiary unit; totals may accompany, never
   substitute for, the one case (`clm-tone-compassionate-identifiable-victim`).
2. **Compassion is depletable and its depletion is contested.** Figley (1995)
   names the cost; Sinclair et al. (2017) caution that "compassion fatigue" is
   often burnout or vicarious trauma. Practical threshold for the card: hold the
   compassionate register for at most one sustained passage per scene or
   ~1,000–1,500 words, then yield to information, action, or another tone
   (`clm-tone-compassionate-compassion-fatigue-yield`).
3. **Melodrama is the tonal ceiling.** Gardner's rule (shared) caps emotion at
   the stakes the text has established; visible tears in the narrator are
   usually a tell that the tone has tipped into performance
   (`clm-tone-compassionate-dosage-saturation`).
4. **Where the tone must yield.** At stakes peaks, drop to the genre engine (a
   thriller's danger, a romance's union) or to plain, near-clinical narration;
   when the subject is the narrator's own loss, use `tone-elegiac`; when the aim
   is general friendliness, use `tone-warm`.
5. **Saturation signals.** (a) Every paragraph opens with a sympathetic
   adjective; (b) two or more consecutive reframes with no new fact or image;
   (c) the narrator's feeling appears in more sentences than the sufferer's
   circumstances; (d) any "we must help them" construction; (e) the compassionate
   passage survives deletion of the named sufferer.

### Evidence-incomplete / limits

- **No direct study tests the compassionate narrative voice itself.** §B
  transfers experimental findings by analogy; the card must state that scope.
- **Compassion-fatigue construct.** Sinclair et al. (2017) show contested
  definitions; the dosage claim is therefore `moderate`, not `strong`.
- **Pity/compassion philosophy.** No single canonical empirical locator; the
  claim rests on interpretive scholarly synthesis (`moderate`).
- **Cross-cultural translation.** Sanskrit/Pali/Chinese terms (`karuna`, `ren`)
  are not exact equivalents of "compassion"; translation limits are stated in §E.
- **Identifiable-victim effect.** Boundary conditions and partial replication
  (Kogut & Ritov 2005) recorded as counterevidence, not suppressed.
