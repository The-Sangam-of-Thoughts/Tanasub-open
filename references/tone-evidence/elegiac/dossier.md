# Elegiac — Research Dossier

- Tone ID: `tone-elegiac`
- Family: Contemplation
- Researched: 2026-09-24
- Evidence status: complete (one sub-section flagged evidence-incomplete, see §G and §I)

Boundary under test (frozen taxonomy): *Faces loss and impermanence in the present.
Differs from `tone-nostalgic` (warmer, past-facing) and `tone-grim` (bleak without
mourning's tenderness).* This dossier supports that boundary and adds one
qualification: elegy also faces non-death losses (place, youth, a vanished world),
and its distinguishing affect is not sadness alone but **tenderness inside sadness**.

## B. Cognitive and psychological foundations

**Mourning vs. melancholia.** Freud (1917) distinguishes mourning — the conscious
working-through of the loss of a loved person or of an abstraction such as home,
liberty, or an ideal — from melancholia, in which the loss is unconscious and the
self is devalued. He notes that mourning, though it departs gravely from normal
life, is not itself pathological. Locator: *Standard Edition* XIV, pp. 237–258,
esp. 243–245. Record: `src-freud-mourning-melancholia-1917` — supplies this
mourning/melancholia distinction as §B's psychological anchor. This
underwrites the elegiac posture as healthy present-tense grief
rather than frozen despair (the boundary against `tone-grim`).

**The dual-process model.** Stroebe and Schut (1999) challenged the "grief work
hypothesis" and modelled adaptive coping as *oscillation* between loss-oriented
and restoration-oriented stressors, adding that adaptive coping requires "dosage
of grieving" — periodic respite from either kind of stressor. Locator: *Death
Studies* 23(3):197–224, abstract and model description. This is the clearest
empirical basis for the card's restraint section: sustained, uninterrupted
mourning is not the healthy norm, and readers tire of it too.

**Continuing bonds.** Klass, Silverman, and Nickman (1996) collected cross-cultural
evidence that the dominant twentieth-century prescription — sever the bond and
"move on" — is not what most bereaved people do or need. Continued connections
(remembering, addressing, keeping linking objects) provide comfort and ease
transition. Locator: *Continuing Bonds*, introduction. This is the psychological
ground for the elegiac device of address to the dead.

**Why sad art is prized.** Menninghaus et al. (2017) propose a distancing-embracing
model: art/representation/fiction schemata create psychological distance and a
sense of safety and control, while compositional interplays of positive and
negative emotion, the aesthetic virtues of the medium, and meaning-making let
recipients "embrace" negative emotion, making reception more intense, moving, and
memorable. Locator: *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 40:e347, §§3–4. This explains
the elegiac paradox — that grief-reading is sought, not merely endured.

**Tenderness inside sadness.** Cova and Deonna (2014) argue that "being moved" is a
distinct emotion, predominantly positive in valence and typically mixed, elicited
prototypically by significant relationship events and critical life events such as
death and birth; it reorders the person's value hierarchy. Locator:
*Philosophical Studies* 169(3):447–466. This is the empirical signature of the
elegiac's *tenderness*, and the mechanism separating it from `tone-grim`.

**Consoling vs. sentimental writing (null + adjacent).** No direct experimental
study was located that compares reader response to consoling versus sentimental
mourning prose. The mechanism is supported by adjacent findings: Gardner's
definition of sentimentality as emotion exceeding what the situation justifies
(shared `src-gardner-art-fiction-1984`, `clm-melodrama-unearned-emotion`), and
Menninghaus et al.'s requirement that embracing be earned through composition and
meaning-making. Flagged as **evidence-incomplete** in §G: the consoling/sentimental
distinction rests on craft authority and aesthetics, not on a controlled study.

## C. Rhetorical and literary tradition

**Classical and pastoral elegy.** Greek *elegeia* named a metre before it named a
mood; the funeral lament and the pastoral elegy (Theocritus through Milton's
*Lycidas*) established the convention-field of shepherd, flower, and seasonal
decay that later elegists inherit and revise (Lambert 1976). The elegiac couplet
and the graveyard mode of Gray's *Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard* (1751)
established the English meditative elegy of mortality.

**Sacks and the compensatory model.** Sacks (1985) reads the English elegy as a
poem of mortal loss that also works toward consolation, and treats its
conventions — pastoral contexts, repetition and refrains, sudden vengeful anger,
deflected sexual power — as constitutive of the work of mourning rather than as
mere ornament (introduction, pp. 1–3). This is the canonical account: the elegy
compensates.

**Ramazani and the melancholic turn.** Ramazani (1994) argues the compensatory
model fits canonical elegies but not the modern elegy, which often refuses
consolation and turns toward masochism, irresolution, irredemption, aggression,
and self-criticism. Locator: *Poetry of Mourning*, introduction/ch. 1. The dossier
therefore treats consolation as *available but optional*, not obligatory.

**Elegy as a mode.** Kennedy (2007) and Weisman (2010) both note the term's
instability: it is used for somber tone generally, for monumentalizing, and for
lament for the dead specifically (Weisman, introduction; she notes the term is
used as a catch-all for somber tone; record `src-weisman-oxford-handbook-elegy-2010`
supplies this field-level definition of elegy's ill-defined scope). Kennedy
traces the elegiac mode across
poetry, fiction, and visual art into digital mourning (ch. 7). This supports
cross-form validity for the tone.

**Condolence and consolation.** The consolatio tradition (Boethius onward) and the
condolence letter provide the rhetorical furniture of comfort; elegy differs in
that the narrator mourns rather than chiefly consoles another (boundary against
`tone-compassionate`).

## D. Craft mechanism inventory

(feeds card §5)

1. **Loss lexicon in the present tense.** Nouns of absence and remainder (ash,
   dust, stone, the empty chair) paired with the present or present-perfect so the
   loss is ongoing. Works because it denies closure; supported by the elegiac
   refusal of consolation (Ramazani) and continuing-bonds theory (Klass et al.).
2. **Monosyllabic plainness and restrained diction.** Short Anglo-Saxon words and
   few emotive adjectives; the affect is carried by situation, not vocabulary.
   Grounded in Gardner's rule that emotion must not exceed the stakes (shared
   source) and in Gray's and Tennyson's plainness.
3. **Apostrophe / second-person address.** Turning to the dead as "you," especially
   mid-poem, keeps the person grammatically alive. Sacks lists address among the
   mourning conventions; Stetkevych documents formulaic address to the slain in
   Arabic *rithāʾ*. Example described, not quoted: al-Khansāʾ's repeated instruction
   to the eye to weep for her brother.
4. **Ubi sunt and seasonal imagery.** "Where are they now," fading light, falling
   flower, last leaf, winter grass — image systems that make impermanence visible.
   Gray's churchyard and the *Heike*'s opening on impermanence are verified
   exemplars (both public-domain; described, not quoted).
5. **Refrain, anaphora, and repetition.** Repeated lines or syntax enact the mind
   returning to the same loss. Sacks names repetition and refrains as
   constitutive; the ghazal's *radīf* (refrain) does this structurally (Faiz).
6. **Falling cadence / extended final clause.** Periodic sentences that settle
   downward, or short declaratives after long ones. Tennyson's *In Memoriam*
   stanza (iambic tetrameter, *abba*) is a verified rhythm of slowed return;
   elegiac couplet tradition supplies the falling close.
7. **Synecdoche and object-memory.** A single kept object (watch, letter, garment)
   stands for the person; links to continuing-bonds "linking objects." Verified in
   memoir practice (Lewis, *A Grief Observed* — copyrighted; described).
8. **Psychic distance: near, then withdrawn.** The voice moves close enough for
   tenderness, then pulls back to abstraction or landscape so the reader is not
   trapped in raw grief. Menninghaus et al.'s distancing factor explains the
   withdrawal; Cova and Deonna's "being moved" explains why closeness still feels
   good.
9. **Mixed light/shadow imagery.** Warmth and cold in one frame (late sun on a
   grave; spring after a death). This is the compositional interplay of positive
   and negative emotion that the distancing-embracing model identifies as central.
10. **The withheld conclusion.** Refusing a consoling summary line; ending on an
    image or question rather than a lesson. Ramazani's modern-elegy irresolution;
    protects against sentimentality and `tone-inspirational` drift.

## E. Cross-cultural survey

**Indian — karuna rasa.** The *Natyashastra* classifies *karuna* (the pathetic /
compassionate sentiment) as one of the eight primary rasas, whose stable emotion
(*sthayi bhava*) is *shoka*, sorrow; it is produced through determinants
(*vibhava*: separation, curses, distress), expressions (*anubhava*: tears,
lamentation, pallor), and transitory states (*vyabhicaribhava*). Locator: shared
`src-natyashastra-ghosh-1951`, ch. 6 (Sentiments), pp. 100–118; cross-link
`clm-rasa-emotional-aesthetic`. Anandavardhana's *Dhvanyaloka* establishes that
suggested emotion (*rasa-dhvani*) is the highest poetic resonance — grief works by
suggestion, not statement (shared `src-anandavardhana-dhvanyaloka-1990`, Uddyota
1). **Translation limit:** Sanskrit technical terms have no clean English
equivalents; "compassion" and "sorrow" both flatten *karuna*, which is closer to
aestheticized grief.

**Japanese — mono no aware.** Motoori Norinaga (1730–1801), in his Genji commentary
*Genji monogatari tama no ogushi* (completed late 1790s), named *mono no aware* —
being moved by things — as the emotional core of literature, and located its most
profound form in the emotion that arises when events do not go as one desires,
as in sadness. He used the concept against Confucian emotional
suppression. Locator: *Motoori Norinaga zenshū* vol. 4, pp. 193–225; Parkes,
*Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, "Japanese Aesthetics" (2005), section on
*mono no aware*. The *Heike monogatari* opening on the impermanence of all things
is the canonical exemplar; Bashō's grass-covered battlefields are the haiku
exemplar. **Caution:** *mono no aware* is not identical to Western elegy — it is
tender acceptance, not protest, and it is frequently religiously grounded.

**Arabic — rithāʾ.** Pre-Islamic and early-Islamic elegy (*rithāʾ*, pl. *marāthī*)
is a ritual, public, formulaic genre; Stetkevych (1993) argues the *qasidah*
traditions are "ritualized in both form and function," and that the elegy's
repetitions and address formulas descend from the wailing-women's *nawḥ*.
Al-Khansāʾ (Tumāḍir bint ʿAmr, d. after 630) wrote over a hundred elegies for her
brothers, and her *Dīwān* was collected by Ibn al-Sikkīt (9th c.); a 1973 English
translation is by Arthur Wormhoudt. Source: Britannica entry (tier 3) plus
Stetkevych (tier 2). **Translation limit:** formulaic repetition that sounds
redundant in English is functional ritual in Arabic.

**Urdu — ghazal grief.** The ghazal sustains *gham* (grief/sorrow) as a permanent
present, using independent couplets, refrain (*radīf*), rhyme (*qāfiya*), and
ambiguity rather than narrative resolution. Faiz Ahmed Faiz (1911–1984) is a modern
master; Agha Shahid Ali's translations in *The Rebel's Silhouette* (1991) render
the grief register in free verse and acknowledge the loss of formal rhyme. Source:
`src-faiz-rebels-silhouette-1991`; Ali's translator's introduction and
Poetry Foundation commentary. **Translation limit:** Ali explicitly notes the
original's sound-patterning cannot be carried into English, so the grief arrives
with the form partly stripped.

No claim here is a cultural universal: the elegiac tone is realised differently in
each tradition, and the shared features (presentness of loss, repetition, address,
tenderness) are family resemblances, not laws.

## G. Failure and misuse research

(feeds card §7)

1. **Sentimentality / unearned emotion.** The governing failure: grief display that
   exceeds the psychologically and causally established stakes. Gardner defines
   sentimentality as demanding an emotional response greater than the situation
   justifies (shared `src-gardner-art-fiction-1984`; cross-link
   **`clm-melodrama-unearned-emotion`**). In elegiac writing this shows as
   declarative weeping ("I sobbed"), stacked emotive adjectives, and summary
   consolations the text has not earned.
2. **Grief-mining / misery branding.** Turning another's death into the writer's
   emotional capital, or repeating a bereavement for effect rather than for
   meaning. This is scoped as **tier 5 / practitioner-criticism**; no independent
   study was located, so it is recorded as a bounded caution, not a tested claim.
3. **Kitsch elegy.** Warm consolation that denies the reality of the loss
   (the "everything happens for a reason" close). Menninghaus et al.'s model
   predicts this fails because the meaning-making is asserted rather than composed.
   **Evidence-incomplete:** the kitsch/profound boundary is described by aesthetics
   (Solomon 2004 defends sentimentality against such charges), not measured.
4. **Frozen melancholy.** Murmuring grief with no oscillation and no tenderness,
   which drifts into `tone-grim` (Freud's melancholia; Stroebe & Schut's absent
   restoration).
5. **Borrowed sorrow.** Using elegiac cadence on losses the text has not made real,
   producing what Ramazani calls unearned or self-regarding mourning.
6. **The consoling summary as `tone-inspirational` drift.** Ending on uplift that
   the elegy's own drift refuses; the composer must choose consolation or
   irresolution, not both by accident.

**Counter-evidence held:** Solomon (2004) argues that sentimentality is often
wrongly condemned and can be ethically serious. The card should treat
"sentimentality" as a *fit-to-stakes* judgment, not a ban on strong feeling.

## H. Dosage and saturation findings

(feeds card §4)

- **Oscillation, not saturation.** The strongest finding is Stroebe and Schut's
  "dosage of grieving": adaptive coping alternates loss-oriented and
  restoration-oriented attention. A draft that stays loss-oriented without recess
  is modelling maladaptive grief and will exhaust the reader.
- **Inverted-U.** Berlyne's psychobiological aesthetics predicts that hedonic value
  is an inverted-U function of arousal potential, with collative variables
  (novelty, complexity, uncertainty) as its main sources (Berlyne 1971;
  *Aesthetics and Psychobiology*, pp. 68–69). Applied to elegy: repeated grief
  cadences raise arousal at first, then flatten. **Scoped caution:** the
  inverted-U is well attested as a heuristic but not uniformly replicated.
- **Reception risk from sheer sadness.** Huron (2011) proposed that sad music can
  be pleasurable via a consoling prolactin response; a direct test (Ladinig et al.
  2021) failed to replicate the prolactin increase. So "sadness is self-limiting
  because it feels good" cannot be asserted; the safer claim is that sadness
  saturates and must be interleaved with restoration or contrast.
- **Observable thresholds for the card author** (writer-inference, bounded):
  keep one dominating elegiac passage per scene/essay section; introduce a
  counter-register (work, memory, humor, landscape, another voice) within roughly
  a page of concentrated mourning; do not stack more than two grief-image
  clusters before an interlude; the elegiac frame must yield at the end to either
  earned acceptance or explicit refusal — never to an unmarked uplift.
- **Context prohibitions.** Do not run `tone-elegiac` over active-threat or
  urgent-action content (it kills urgency); do not use it to console a real person
  in a service message unless the brief asks for condolence, where
  `tone-compassionate` is primary.
